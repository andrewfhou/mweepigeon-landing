### How does the design team translate Universes Beyond characters into Universes Within characters? I love the direction of characters like Zethi and Rashelle in particular, could one of those be the example?
**DK:** I did work on the Through the Omenpaths set, which was actually a lot of fun. When we get these characters and we get these really specific mechanics that we can't change, having those restrictions opens up creative opportunities, because we're starting mechanic first and trying to theorize, one, what plane could this go on—so I feel like a lot of things start there, there's so many combinations that are plane specific, so you want to start there and decide what the equivalent could be. It becomes this never ending puzzle, really, where we completely strip away what the original was so it doesn't distract us. It's very much a puzzle, we go mechanic forwards, we look at the colors, we're comparing to existing Magic sets and see where it can go. To get these oddballs with zero context was almost like playing a game

**Neale:** I was on the second Universes Within treatment (Street Fighter translation) and the philosophy has changed so much over time. When I started it was, take every one of these characters and put them on one plane, figure out how you can match all those typelines, and make every mechanic make sense. So for that drop, we put everyone on Dominaria, and the philosophy has definitely evolved to where we can actually map characters and their mechanics to the planes that inspired them. So if we saw Landfall on a UB character, we could be like 'well could this be on Zendikar'. And that's a really interesting question that I think has made our Universes Within stuff resonate so much more

**Amanda:** When I approach those cards from a naming perspective is how I approach any other card we create in Magic IP. What is the art saying, what is the direction the mechanics are saying, and what is the story we're trying to tell.
*// some fun discussion about trying to develop flavor, both in-universe and Universes Beyond, for generic draft chaff shitters*

### We're out of questions, so... Neale, do you have any questions?
**Neale:** What? Me? *Moi?*

**Crystal:** Do we want to give a little bit of insight into the creative process for a Magic set

**Neale:** Internally we have processes that are outside our creative team where a larger team full of leadership and other folks and creatives will come up with our lineup of sets. Example, Bloomburrow used to be called something called "Beast Nations", just vague, anthro stuff. So we really start on these big, blue sky high concept places and we whittle down through testing/iterating—thinking like, no it's actually not cool if our anthro world is actually an apocalypse, that was a real pitch I sent to Emily Teng, it was Stalker but animals, not very fun, not very cozy.

**Neale:** But we'll sort of go and test and iterate, drill down with game designers, developing mechanics in tandem with what we're doing. And then, when it all culminates, we have this thing called a concept push, and that's the fulfillment of all that early iteration and testing and brainstorming and coalescing into this one big grand moment where we get ~6-10 artists

**Gray:** It's about 9 nowadays, I think. We have our small internal team and we get a bunch of external artists to come in. The art directory and narrative designer have set up what we know about the plane, the set, e.g we have 5 factions and I've put together this mood board. We want it to look like XYZ but not like ABC. Let's figure it out, we have 3 weeks. We draw a lot—so much—during the concept push. What is costuming look like, what does casting magic look like, how is that different from this other set we've done 10 years ago, it can't be too close. We're trying to figure out what makes this set unique, what makes this set look like Magic but also communicate what we're trying to get it to communicate. Then, all of that goes into a big world guide and that goes out to artists who are doing cards

**DK:** At this point, if I'm just concepting on a set, we have our world guide and a lot of time the concepters have contributed to that as well. We focus on how we're trying to populate the world, because not everything that's in the world guide makes it into the card set. It's a map to guide us. We try to map what about the world the players to see while they're playing, it's important to us that everything makes sense when it comes together. We try to anticipate future things, we try to be considerate of creative text, we want it to be almost self explanatory on the card and not contradict the mechanics. We work with game mechanics, like, 'Hey, I think this would explain a crucial detail of the world, is it cool if I change the creature type'. And we have a little bit of an editing process to finalize things

**Neale:** Just for clarity, after we're done writing the world guide, the next stage of development that we call concepting is when the worldbuilding lead and a team of writers go in and write an art brief for each card. That includes what the art needs to look like and all these worldbuilding details, and then that art gets commissioned by the lead AD and the AD team.

**Crystal:** Universes Beyond it's very similar, but the biggest difference is that we don't necessarily need to introduce you to each of these characters. It's nice if the art is evocative and iconic, but it's like when we put Chandra on a card. People know who Chandra, Bolas, is, you don't need the card to explain that to you. So we want the card to feel very genuine and true to that person, but it doesn't need to be explanatory or an example of it. So we want to focus more on the aspect of the character being showcased mechanically and the art to back this up. Like, is this a picture of Leonardo leading, or is this a picture of him training, etc.

**Crystal:** Working with partners is a huge part of the job, every partner has a different process for licensing. It's an issue of where are you allowed to take reference from—some are really good about you just grabbing it offline, some want you to only pull it from these specific books or movies, or from their specific content database that they have—so a lot of it is digging through to find very specific references. And there's a lot of back and forth, after we write the art brief, we'll take it back to the partner and they'll approve it or provide feedback.

**Amanda:** Creative text is the last stage in the creative lifecycle of a card. A lot of it is working off the foundation that the narrative designer set. We approach all our sets and come up with core themes and tones, moods that we're trying to hit. That's true for MIP but also UB, what are the goals we're trying to hit with this set. A lot of my work is translating those goals, how they appear in the creative text of the cards. I work with an external team of writers for each set, each set is new and has different needs, and we work card-by-card. On the UB side, I know for them they're looking for folks that are really ingrained in that IP, that know the material and can make all the references. We love to find fans, of Magic too. I make sure that the creative text that doesn't conflict; sometimes we have late mechanic changes, crazy art that changes, and we have to solve on the card level, and then it goes off to editing!
